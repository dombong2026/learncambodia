> A website reported that bus use had fallen by a third after the city raised fares. Its chart used ticket sales for only three routes during roadworks. On two of those routes, replacement minibuses accepted the same tickets but recorded trips separately. The transport office said it did not yet have figures for the network as a whole. The site kept the chart and revised the headline to say that recorded ticket sales had dropped on three routes.
What was the main weakness in the original headline?|It extended limited route data to overall bus use|It quoted the transport office incorrectly|It reported journeys by train
Why should the replacement minibus figures matter?|Some actual bus trips were counted separately|The minibuses charged the wrong fares|Their drivers refused all tickets
What exact comparison could the chart support?|Recorded ticket sales on the three routes|All journeys before and after fares rose|Roadworks costs across the city
What remained unknown when the headline changed?|The network-wide figures|The number of routes on the chart|Whether the roadworks took place
How did the revision improve the story?|It matched the claim to what was measured|It added minibus journeys to the chart|It explained who decided to raise fares
> Two outlets interviewed the same parent about plans to extend the school day. The school said the proposal was still under review. A local reporter then interviewed families on several bus routes and asked what timetable changes would make the plan workable.

How did the local reporter broaden the evidence?|By interviewing families on several routes|By quoting the same line a third time|By publishing an official opinion anonymously
What is the main lesson of the example?|Accurate words can mislead when context is missing|Anonymous quotations are always fabricated|One direct quotation proves how most parents feel

What additional evidence would test the scheme’s goal?|Records of missed appointments|The number of forms printed alone|The online form’s font size
