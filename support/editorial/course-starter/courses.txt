GE|A1|foundations|Everyday English A1: First Conversations|Introduce yourself, exchange simple personal information, and make a short everyday request.
Starting a conversation|Greetings and introductions|Use “Hello, I’m Dara. What’s your name?” and “Nice to meet you.” For a more formal first meeting, choose “Good morning.”|Introduce yourself twice, once to a classmate and once to a new colleague. Include your name and one question.
Sharing simple details|Numbers and contact information|Say phone numbers one digit at a time. Ask “Could you repeat the last two digits?” before writing down a number you did not hear clearly.|Invent a phone number and spell a fictional name for a partner. Compare what they wrote with your original.
Everyday requests|Ordering and asking politely|“Could I have a glass of water, please?” names both the item and the quantity. Say “That’s all, thank you” to finish.|Practise ordering one item from a three-item menu. Change the quantity on your second attempt and listen as your partner repeats the order.

GE|A2|foundations|Everyday English A2: Plans and Messages|Arrange times, change plans politely, and confirm details in a short written message.
Making plans|Suggesting and agreeing|“Are you free at three?” asks about availability. If the time is inconvenient, follow with “Could we meet at four instead?”|Use two different calendars to find a shared time. Each person should propose at least one alternative.
Describing a change|Reasons and time expressions|“I can’t meet on Friday because I’m working late” gives a reason before a new proposal. Compare “on Friday” with “at four.”|Rewrite three messages using the right time phrase. Read one aloud and have a partner repeat the proposed date and time.
Confirming arrangements|Three-sentence follow-up messages|Name the agreed place and time, then state what you will bring or do. End with a question only when something remains uncertain.|Write a follow-up message for a study session. Ask a partner to identify the time, location, and any next action.

ESL|A2|community|Community English: Appointments, Notices and Directions|Handle common transactions in English-speaking settings, from appointment calls to local notices and directions.
Making appointments|Opening a phone call|Begin with your purpose. “I’d like to make an appointment for next week” works before you know what times are available.|Role-play a call where the first time offered is unavailable. Repeat the final date and time before ending the call.
Reading local notices|Find the action first|For “Main entrance closed until noon. Use the north gate,” identify the alternate entrance and when the closure ends.|Write a notice about a room change and ask a partner to say where they would go and when.
Navigating an area|Use recognizable landmarks|A useful route names a turning point and a landmark. “Turn right at the pharmacy” is easier to check than “Turn right over there.”|Draw four landmarks on a map. Give a three-step route without showing the map and compare it with your partner’s drawing.

EFL|B1|conversation|English Conversation Practice: Keep the Discussion Going|Build confidence using English outside class through follow-up questions, short narratives, and group decisions.
Asking follow-up questions|Turn an answer into a conversation|If someone says “I went to the market,” ask “What did you buy?” using one detail from the reply before changing topics.|Hold a one-minute conversation. Replace one yes-or-no question with a follow-up that begins with what or how.
Telling a short story|Organize time and place|Introduce the people and place first. Then use “at first,” “after that,” and “finally” to help the listener track events.|Tell a familiar story in ninety seconds. Ask a partner to identify the beginning, turning point, and ending.
Taking decisions in groups|Include each speaker|A clear discussion task invites everyone to offer a preference and a reason before the group decides.|Choose an outing from three options. Rotate moderator, speaker, and recorder roles and confirm one shared decision.

BE|B1|workplace|Workplace English: Emails and Meeting Follow-Ups|Write clear requests, take action-focused meeting notes, and send concise follow-ups.
Workplace requests|Ask for one concrete action|“Please review the schedule and send your changes by Thursday at noon” gives the reader a task, document, and deadline.|Rewrite “Can you check this soon?” in two ways, one for a colleague and one for a new client.
Meeting notes|Separate decisions from discussion|Write “Decision: use the smaller room” separately from “Open: who will bring the projector?” Give each assigned task an owner.|Read a short fictional meeting transcript and write a recap with one decision, one action, and one open question.
Follow-up messages|Make next steps easy to find|Put urgent actions near the start. List a name beside each assigned task and include a deadline only when it was agreed.|Send a three-line recap of a role-play meeting. Ask another participant to point out anything unconfirmed.

BE|B2|workplace|English for Workplace Presentations and Discussions|Present a clear recommendation, explain figures, and respond politely to challenges.
Structuring a presentation|Give the point early|Open with your recommendation and tell listeners what evidence you will show. A one-sentence signpost helps them organize the details.|Prepare a one-minute introduction for a change to a work process. Ask a listener to state the recommendation after the first sentence.
Explaining figures|Compare like with like|“We handled 30 requests this week, up from 20 last week” is clear because both numbers use the same period and unit.|Choose three figures from an invented report. Give one comparison and state why the change matters.
Handling disagreement|Acknowledge and address the concern|“That’s a reasonable concern. Could we try a smaller pilot first?” acknowledges the objection and proposes an alternative.|Answer two objections to a fictional plan. Make sure each answer responds to the specific concern.

EAP|B2|academic|Academic English: Reading, Evidence and Paragraphs|Read short academic texts, paraphrase evidence accurately, and build clear academic paragraphs.
Reading research|Find the question, method and result|In an abstract, distinguish what authors asked, what they did, and what they found. Mark missing information to look for in the full text.|Select an open-access abstract and write one line for each of those three parts.
Using sources|Paraphrase with attribution|Put the source aside after reading. Rewrite the idea in a new sentence structure, compare for accuracy, and retain the citation.|Paraphrase two sentences from a source you can cite. Keep any qualification that limits the original claim.
Building paragraphs|Connect a claim to its evidence|State the point, give evidence, and explain the link. A statistic or quotation needs an explanation of why it supports the claim.|Write one paragraph with a claim, evidence, and explanation. Underline each part with a different mark.

ESP|B1|hospitality|English for Hotel Reception and Guest Service|Conduct check-ins, explain services, and respond to guest requests in clear hospitality English.
Guest arrivals|Check-in language and confirmations|Ask for the booking name, confirm the room details discreetly, and explain the next step. “Breakfast is served downstairs from seven” gives a time and place.|Role-play a check-in where the guest’s room preference differs from the booking. Confirm the preference before discussing options.
Hotel information|Describe schedules and directions|Give a location and a landmark together. “The lifts are on the left, just past the seating area” lets a guest check the route.|Use a simple hotel plan to answer three guest questions about locations and opening times.
Guest problems|Explain a delay and offer options|“We expect an update by three, and I’ll call your room then” makes the next point of contact explicit.|Role-play a delayed check-in. Offer a reasonable waiting option and ask the guest to confirm how they would like an update.

TOEIC|B1|exams|TOEIC Preparation: Workplace Listening and Reading|Practise listening for changes and finding written evidence in original workplace scenarios.
Workplace announcements|Listen for updated details|Phrases such as “instead” and “from now on” often signal that an earlier time or location has changed.|Have a partner read an original announcement that reschedules a meeting. Identify the final room and time.
Short workplace texts|Scan notices and schedules|Decide whether the question asks for a time, person, place, or action. Then scan for the corresponding detail.|Write a short staff notice with two times. Ask a partner to identify which applies to a named task.
Reviewing answers|Mark the supporting evidence|A choice that uses familiar words can still conflict with the passage. Underline the phrase that proves your answer before considering alternatives.|Write one correct option and two plausible distractors for a fictional email. Explain why each distractor fails.

TOEFL|B2|exams|TOEFL Preparation: Academic English Skills|Practise extracting lecture ideas, summarizing viewpoints, and giving organized spoken responses.
Lecture listening|Separate the main idea from an example|Use “for example” as a cue to label the illustration. Keep the central claim above the details in your notes.|Watch an openly available short lecture. Write the main idea and two examples without copying entire sentences.
Academic discussions|Summarize two viewpoints|Name the shared topic first. Then describe each speaker’s reason using your own words and a neutral tone.|Ask two partners to debate a campus issue. Write one sentence for each position and confirm you represented it accurately.
Speaking practice|Answer, reason, example|State your answer immediately, give a reason, and develop one example before adding another idea.|Record a response about a course you enjoyed. Mark the moment the listener hears your main reason and revise if it comes too late.

IELTS|B2|exams|IELTS Academic Preparation: Writing and Speaking|Practise explaining data, developing an essay position, and expanding spoken answers with specific details.
Visual information|Describe the main pattern in a chart|Name the time period and unit, then group similar movements. An overview can highlight a meaningful contrast before listing figures.|Draw two lines moving differently across four months. Write a two-sentence overview and one numerical comparison.
Essay development|Support a clear position|Decide what the question asks before outlining a position. Give each paragraph one reason and a relevant example.|Draft a response about public transport. Ask a partner to identify your position and match each paragraph to a reason.
Speaking development|Add a relevant detail|A brief response can expand naturally with a reason and a specific example. Avoid giving a memorized speech that drifts from the question.|Record two answers on an everyday topic. Replace general adjectives in one answer with concrete examples.

IELTS|B1|exams|IELTS Foundations: Listening and Reading Practice|Build the habits of tracking corrected details, identifying paraphrases, and checking answers against evidence.
Listening accuracy|Track final decisions|When a speaker says “actually” or “let’s make that,” listen for the updated detail. Keep suggestions separate from confirmed information.|Write and read a thirty-second conversation with two time changes. Record the final time from memory.
Reading accuracy|Recognize when a claim is too strong|A passage saying “many residents” does not support a choice that says “all residents.” Pay attention to degree and limits.|Write three statements about a short text. Mark each as supported, contradicted, or undetermined and justify your choice.
Practice review|Keep an error log|Classify each missed answer by cause, such as missed detail, wrong word match, or earlier information superseded by a later correction.|Review five completed questions. Choose one repeated cause and design a short practice task for it.

PTE|B2|exams|PTE Academic Preparation: Spoken and Written Fluency|Practise reading aloud, describing visuals, and summarizing short texts using original exercises.
Speaking clearly|Read in meaningful groups|Pause between thought groups and keep small words with the phrases they introduce. Clear phrasing supports understanding without forcing an unnatural pace.|Record a paragraph twice. Compare where you paused and whether each phrase carried a complete idea.
Describing visuals|Overview before numbers|Identify the topic and make one useful comparison. Include units where they matter rather than listing every number on the page.|Sketch a bar chart and record a brief explanation. Ask a listener to describe the main pattern from your recording.
Written summaries|Keep the main idea and its qualification|Find what the writer claims and retain a limitation only if removing it changes the meaning. Use your own sentence structure.|Summarize a short passage in one sentence. Check that a reader could understand it without the original.

DET|B1|exams|Duolingo English Test Preparation: Short Responses|Develop concise spoken and written answers and practise describing images with visible evidence.
Oral fluency|Make a short reply specific|Name one activity, explain why it matters, and give a recent example. This often produces a clearer reply than listing several unrelated ideas.|Answer the same prompt twice, once generally and once with one detailed example. Compare the recordings.
Image description|Separate evidence from inference|Start with the most visible action and location. Use “might” or “appears to” for a detail you cannot confirm from the image.|Choose an image without its caption. Ask someone to underline the details they can verify visually.
Focused writing|Check each sentence against the prompt|Underline the topic and the action requested. A question asking for an advantage can be answered with one developed reason and example.|Write one paragraph about group study. Remove a sentence that does not support the main idea and check the revised flow.

OET|B2|exams|OET Preparation: Healthcare Communication in English|Practise clear professional language through fictional appointment, handover, and patient communication exercises.
Opening a conversation|State your purpose in plain English|“I’d like to check that I understand your main concern” gives the other person a reason to speak. Use plain language for non-technical listeners.|Role-play a fictional appointment opening. Ask your partner to identify the question that invited their explanation.
Writing a brief handover|Separate facts from interpretation|Start with the purpose, then include relevant dates and the speaker’s own account. Label any uncertainty instead of passing it on as a confirmed fact.|Write a short handover about a fictional appointment reschedule. Remove a detail that does not help the recipient.
Checking understanding|Use a teach-back question|“Just so I know I explained it clearly, how would you describe the next step?” checks the explanation without blaming the listener. Use only fictional language scenarios.|Explain a pretend follow-up booking process and ask your partner to repeat the steps in their own words.

CELPIP|B2|exams|CELPIP Preparation: Community and Workplace English|Practise giving recommendations, writing a request, and listening for confirmed arrangements.
Giving advice|Compare options using shared criteria|Use the same factors for each option, such as cost and convenience. Explain which factor matters most to your listener.|Compare two fictional venues for a neighborhood event and recommend one with two reasons.
Community messages|Make a practical request|“The meeting room is locked before class. Could it be opened fifteen minutes earlier?” states the problem and an action that could fix it.|Write a message about a fictional community facility. Include the location, impact, and a feasible request.
Listening for agreement|Distinguish suggestions from decisions|Phrases such as “That works” and “Let’s go with that” can indicate an agreed time. Record it separately from earlier proposals.|Create a dialogue with three suggested dates. Ask a partner to note only the date everyone accepted.

FCE|B2|exams|B2 First Preparation: Writing and Speaking|Develop organized B2-level paragraphs, compare photographs, and respond to a prompt in an informal email.
Essay practice|Develop distinct reasons|Plan two reasons and give one example for each. An effective transition shows how ideas are related rather than decorating the paragraph.|Write two paragraphs on homework policy. Ask a classmate to identify the different reason in each.
Comparing pictures|Describe, compare and infer carefully|Use “both” to name a similarity and “whereas” to show a difference. Use “might” for a guess you cannot verify.|Compare two photos of study settings. Give one similarity, one difference, and one tentative inference.
Informal email|Answer every requested point|Group related details in a friendly tone. End with a relevant question rather than a generic closing line.|Respond to a friend’s three questions about a visit. Check that each question received a specific answer.

CAE|C1|exams|C1 Advanced Preparation: Argument and Register|Synthesize viewpoints, develop nuanced arguments, and write with a register suited to the audience.
Academic and professional arguments|Use concessions to set up contrast|“Although online meetings reduce travel, technical decisions can be easier to discuss in person” shows where the emphasis lies.|Write a paragraph about remote study with a concession and check that the counterpoint is represented fairly.
Reading across texts|Synthesize rather than list|If two writers agree about an outcome but disagree about the cause, begin with shared ground and organize the contrast by idea.|Compare two short essays on one topic. Name a shared claim and a difference, citing a phrase for each.
Register in proposals|Use measurable actions and clear audiences|Use concrete verbs and identify who would carry out each recommendation. “Train new mentors during orientation” is easier to assess than “improve preparation.”|Write a proposal for a study group. Replace two vague requests with specific actions.

TEFL|-|teaching|Teaching English: Communicative Lesson Design|Plan goal-driven speaking tasks, check instructions, and use classroom evidence to improve feedback.
Communicative tasks|Create a reason to speak|Ask pairs to choose a meeting time from different timetables. Both learners need to exchange information before they can decide.|Design one task with an information gap and a clear decision. Check whether each learner has information the other needs.
Checking instructions|Ask observable questions|“How many ideas will you write?” checks a specific part of the task. “Does everyone understand?” often produces little evidence.|Write three quick checks for a pair activity and have a colleague answer from your instructions.
Feedback after fluency practice|Use useful anonymous examples|Listen during the task, collect one successful phrase and one recurring difficulty, and delay most correction until the conversation ends.|Observe a mock discussion and prepare two examples. Ask learners to improve one and then try a short second round.

TEYL|-|young-learners|Teaching Young Learners: Stories, Movement and Games|Build short, age-appropriate activities using movement, picture books, and spoken routines.
Actions and movement|Connect words to observable actions|Model a few commands such as “stand,” “turn,” and “point.” After several repetitions, see whether learners respond without copying gestures.|Plan a two-minute activity with three commands. Change their order and note which learners respond to the language alone.
Stories and prediction|Use pictures to invite short replies|Pause on an illustration and ask “What might happen next?” Accept a short reply and model a longer version where helpful.|Choose three illustrations from a book you can use and write an age-appropriate prediction question for each.
Vocabulary games|Give children a reason to use the word|Place different pictures with each partner. One names an object while the other identifies whether they have a matching image.|Plan a six-picture game and rotate roles. Finish by asking each learner to use one target word in a new example.
